# Quadrant interpolation and better Z interpolation lead the field

The published alternatives to our tracker are few, and two of them are worth trying first. For XY, **quadrant interpolation (QI)** (van Loenhout et al. 2012) is the only method with demonstrated low pixel bias on magnetic-tweezers ring images at low magnification, and it has an MIT-licensed reference implementation (QTrk). For Z, the strongest candidate is not a new estimator but a **better sub-slice interpolator** on our existing per-bead lookup table (LUT): Kovari et al. 2019 report spline plus Gauss-Newton refinement to better than 0.05 of a slice step, while a parabola near the minimum is unreliable under noise. The FFT-phasor method (3DPT, Brouwer et al. 2020) is a robustness option rather than a precision upgrade, since its own authors say a well-run LUT tracker is faster and appears more accurate. Our current method is the classic Gosse & Croquette style (mirrored cross profiles for XY, a band-passed complex radial profile matched against a 60-slice stack for Z), which the field has used for 20 years. The Å-level numbers in the literature come from kHz cameras, deep pixel wells and high magnification, not from better estimators, so they are not targets for our 8-bit, 90-150 Hz rig. The only fair test is relative: every candidate and the current method run on identical frames, first on simulated images with known truth (bias, crosstalk, linearity), then on our recorded 5-bead file (noise via Allan deviation, agreement, lost beads, time). Two facts settled after the first draft (owner, 2026-10-22): the recording was taken with a **50x oil objective** (camera SP-5000M-USB, 5.0 µm pixels, binning 2, so about **200 nm per image pixel** nominally; the calibration file's own `pixel distance` is 84 nm, which disagrees and is an open owner question, see [rig-facts.md](../../rig-facts.md)), and every measurement includes non-paramagnetic streptavidin polystyrene **reference beads** stuck to the surface; in the recorded file this is **bead 1** (see "Our recorded baseline" below).

## Our method sits in the classic mirrored-profile family

Our tracker is described authoritatively in decision 0003 ([0003-tracking-port-and-swappable-stages.md](../../decisions/0003-tracking-port-and-swappable-stages.md)). Per bead and frame it cuts a 120 px square around the previous position. For XY it averages a horizontal and a vertical band through the centre into two 1D profiles, removes the baseline, applies a window, autocorrelates each profile with its mirror image by FFT, fits a parabola to the peak, and removes pixel-discretisation bias in three passes (three inverse FFTs and three parabola fits per axis, with two phase shifts of the spectrum between them; the original VI's 3-frame stacked sequence, `ref_numpy.find_avg_profile_center`). It then builds a radial profile I(r) around the new centre, splitting each pixel between two radius bins. For Z it mirrors I(r), applies a cosine band-pass in Fourier space to get a complex ("corkscrew") signal, picks the closest of the bead's **60 calibration slices of 0.1 µm** by least squares, and refines with a weighted quadratic fit of the phase differences to the five neighbouring slices. The Python port with numba reproduces LabVIEW within 1e-6 px and 1e-4 µm on the recorded fixture and meets the 6 ms per frame budget ([offline-core results](../../results/2026-10-22-offline-core.md)).

The Gosse & Croquette 2002 paper itself could not be read: its PMC PDF sits behind a browser check, and the abstract mentions only the feedback-coupled tracking system, trap stiffness and force range, with no XY or Z precision ([Gosse & Croquette 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0006-3495(02)75672-5)). **Every claim about that paper's own numbers, filter band, slice spacing and interpolation formula is therefore unverified.** What is verified is that later primary papers describe the same XY principle: Lansdorp et al. 2013 cross-correlate horizontal and vertical line profiles with their mirror images and fit a second-degree polynomial for sub-pixel accuracy ([Lansdorp 2013](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4802678)), and Ribeck & Saleh 2008 used a LabVIEW routine "similar to" Gosse & Croquette, with 64-128 px windows ([Ribeck & Saleh 2008](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.2981687)). The Croquette lab's later Fourier-phase localisation, read in full, uses the same idea as our Z refinement: phase differences of Fourier modes against a reference, weighted by modulus, with the shift read from a fitted slope ([Rieu et al., arXiv 2008.02898](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02898)). The mirror trick needs no template and only assumes the pattern is symmetric, which is why it has lasted.

The method's known weakness is **pixel bias**. Pixel-grid bias is periodic in the sub-pixel position and arises because the sampled image contains spatial frequencies above half the sampling rate; kernel and correlation methods inherit it through the sub-pixel peak fit ([van Loenhout 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpj.2012.03.073)). In van Loenhout's simulations at SNR 30 the 1D mirrored cross-correlation (XCOR) had bias that fell only slowly with magnification and exceeded its scatter at most magnifications, and on real multiplexed data at 25x it showed "large discrete steps" and overestimated DNA length (2.42 ± 0.14 µm versus 2.33 ± 0.18 µm for QI, 2.26 µm expected) ([van Loenhout 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3353059/)). Our extra phase-shift passes are a bias correction that the plain XCOR in that study did not have, so how much of this bias remains in our tracker is an open, measurable question, not a given. Its importance is real: at about 200 nm per pixel (50x, binning 2) we are close to van Loenhout's ~247 nm/px case, the low-magnification regime where pixel bias matters most. ("16X" in the recording folder name is the DNA construct's name, not a magnification.)

**Our recorded baseline** (computed 2026-10-22 from the original program's own output `tra002-000`, 10,043 frames at 90 Hz; frame-to-frame noise = standard deviation of successive differences / √2):

## Quadrant interpolation is the XY method to beat

The table below compares the XY estimators on the evidence in the notes. Precision numbers come from very different instruments and are not comparable across rows; they show what each method has achieved, not what it would give us.

| Method | Principle | Reported XY result (conditions) | Speed reported | Bias / robustness | Code and licence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ours (Gosse & Croquette style) | 1D mirrored profiles, FFT autocorrelation, parabola, 3 phase-shift iterations | Same family: 1.3 Å RMS lateral, fixed bead pair, 63 nm/px, 2.5 kHz ([Huhle 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms6885)) | 80 beads < 6 ms/frame on 12 threads (our port) | Pixel bias at low magnification; XCOR bias independent of SNR | Ours |
| Centre of mass (COM) | Intensity-weighted mean | Never reaches QI bias level ([van Loenhout 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpj.2012.03.073)) | Fastest | Most biased; sensitive to uneven background and bead asymmetry; 0 of 245 beads fittable at 25x | Trivial; first guess only |
| Quadrant interpolation (QI) | Polar resampling with bilinear interpolation (δr ≈ 1/3 px), four quadrant radial profiles, FFT mirror correlation, 5-point parabola | Sub-1 nm XY at 30x (~247 nm/px), simulation at SNR 30; 228 beads at 58 Hz with ~1 nm SD ([van Loenhout 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpj.2012.03.073); [Cnossen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4898178)) | 10 ms per 128² ROI on a 2-GHz Core2 in LabVIEW (2012); GPU real-time (2014) | "Not bias-limited" from 30x; bias below scatter at all SNRs; scatter near the Cramér-Rao bound (abstract) | QTrk, MIT ([github.com/jcnossen/qtrk](https://github.com/jcnossen/qtrk)) |
| FFT-phasor (3DPT) | 2D FFT correlation with synthetic single-frequency complex references; amplitude peak gives XY | ~0.2 nm at 30 Hz from PSD; fixed beads σxy 0.6 nm ([Brouwer 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpj.2020.01.015)) | Up to 10,000 ROIs/s at 100² px on a 10-core CPU; 2-5x slower than LUT | Robust to Poisson noise, gradients up to 20%, ROI offset up to 30%; references must be retuned with pixel size | LabVIEW on GitHub; repository and licence unverified |
| Radial symmetry | Analytic least-squares centre of gradient lines | Near theoretical limits, orders of magnitude faster than fitting, on fluorescent spots and colloids ([Parthasarathy 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nmeth.2071)) | ~10 µs for 9x9 px | Claimed low neighbour sensitivity; **never validated on MT rings** | Author's MATLAB page |
| 2D Gaussian fit | Model fit | Best for point sources, robust at low SNR ([Cheezum 2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0006-3495(01)75884-5)) | Slow | Wrong model for defocused rings | Not recommended |
| Holographic (HoloMiP) | Holographic reconstruction plus 3D parabolic masking, no LUT | Claimed better precision; no number extracted ([HoloMiP 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9300656/)) | Not extracted | Handles overlapping patterns | Not checked |
| Deep learning (CenterNet) | Network trained on synthetic bead images | ~48 nm horizontal, ~199 nm z, near focus ([Sensors 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11054292/)) | Not relevant | Two orders of magnitude worse than classical methods | Not recommended |

QI wins on principle because it samples every pixel through interpolation rather than a subset of lines, and because the polar grid removes the pixel lattice from the correlation ([van Loenhout 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3353059/)). Its main experimental evidence, more fittable tethers and correct DNA lengths at 25x, is exactly the regime we suspect we are in. Its cost is the polar resampling: the 2012 LabVIEW figure of 10 ms per 128² ROI is far too slow for our 12,000 ROIs/s (80 beads at 150 Hz) single-threaded, but Cnossen's GPU version tracked about 13,000 ROIs/s on 2014 consumer hardware, so a numba-parallel or RTX 2060 version is plausible though unmeasured. QTrk's chain is COM, then 1D cross-correlation, then QI; MagTrack's documentation shows QI-style refinement iterated about three times, but the 2012 paper gives a single correction pass and no iteration count was found ([MagTrack guide](https://magtrack.readthedocs.io/en/v0.7.7/user_guide.html)). A naming trap matters here: **MagTrack's `qi()` is quadratic interpolation, not quadrant interpolation** ([MagTrack core.py](https://github.com/7jameslondon/MagTrack/blob/master/magtrack/core.py)).

XY quality also decides Z quality. Van Loenhout found that Z error is dominated by propagated XY error, that feeding QI's centre into the older Z trackers brought them to QI-level Z, and that a non-interpolating radial profile with 1-px bins was more than ten times worse in Z under bead motion. This crosstalk is invisible on fixed beads and appears only with moving ones ([van Loenhout 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3353059/)); Carlucci & Thomas likewise saw errors for mobile beads about ten times those of stuck beads ([Carlucci & Thomas](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10350879)). Our radial profile already splits each pixel between two bins, which is a form of interpolation, so the gain from QI-style polar resampling of the radial profile is again a question to measure. All symmetry-based methods fail once the innermost rings of neighbours overlap; Ribeck & Saleh needed 5-8 µm spacing for 1 µm beads ([Ribeck & Saleh 2008](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.2981687)).

## Z gains come from interpolation, not new estimators

Every serious MT tracker in the notes still reads Z from a radial profile matched to a calibration table, and they differ mainly in **how they interpolate between slices**. Lansdorp used 100 nm steps with least squares and polynomial fitting ([Lansdorp 2013](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4802678)); Huhle used 25 nm steps and reached 1.8 Å axial RMS at 2.5 kHz ([Huhle 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4338538/)); Brouwer's LUT baseline used an RMS minimum over 64 profiles with a quadratic refinement ([Brouwer 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7202940/)). Kovari et al. 2019, read in full, turn the LUT into smoothing splines and solve for z by Gauss-Newton minimisation; in simulation this beat discrete and quadratic matching at every noise level, reached interpolation accuracy better than 0.05 step, returns a per-frame σz from the Jacobian, and runs up to about 1 kHz ([Kovari 2019](https://doi.org/10.1364/OE.27.029875)). The same paper shows why our per-bead stacks matter: using another bead's LUT gave errors of ±0.1 µm for 1.04 µm beads against 5-10 nm with the bead's own LUT.

| Method | Principle | Reported Z result (conditions) | Speed | Bias / robustness | Code and licence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ours | Band-passed complex profile; least-squares slice; weighted quadratic fit of phase differences over 5 neighbours; 0.1 µm slices | Not verified for G&C 2002 | Within our 6 ms budget | Possible periodic error with a 100 nm period; unmeasured | Ours |
| LUT + quadratic minimum (Lansdorp, QTrk, Brouwer baseline) | Squared-residual curve over slices, parabola at minimum | ~1 Å at 100 Hz, stuck bead, reference-subtracted ([Lansdorp 2013](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4802678)) | Fast | Quadratic vertex unreliable under noise ([Kovari 2019](https://doi.org/10.1364/OE.27.029875)); Cnossen reports an unquantified axial bias | Lansdorp CUDA (MIT); QTrk (MIT) |
| LUT + spline + Gauss-Newton (Kovari) | Continuous LUT, iterative z solve, per-frame σz | < 0.05 step interpolation error; 5-10 nm with own LUT, 20 nm steps | Up to ~1 kHz | Best-documented sub-slice interpolator | MATLAB, [ExtrasToolbox](https://github.com/dkovari/ExtrasToolbox) and [figshare](https://figshare.com/articles/Radial_Symmetry_LUT_Tracking_Code/8317136); licence not checked |
| FFT-phasor (3DPT) | Phase at the correlation peak, polynomial phase-to-z map, no LUT | 2.4 nm at 30 Hz with 5 references; fixed beads σz 1.6 nm; polynomial residual < 15 nm over 10 µm ([Brouwer 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpj.2020.01.015)) | 2-5x slower than LUT | No outliers where LUT showed some under Poisson noise; calibration carries between beads; LUT "appears more accurate" under good conditions | LabVIEW; licence unverified |
| Lorenz-Mie fitting | Physical scattering model, no calibration | Systematic z errors of hundreds of nm for paramagnetic beads ([Brouwer 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpj.2020.01.015)) | Seconds per ROI | Inhomogeneous beads break the model | Not for live use |
| Stereo darkfield interferometry (SDI) | Fringe separation, linear in z | 0.2 nm photon-noise floor, 6 µm range ([Rieu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02898)) | Not relevant | Needs Fourier-plane optics | Hardware change, out of scope |
| Deep learning | CNN regression | No verified MT result with nm precision | — | Immature for MT | — |

Our Z refinement is already closer in spirit to the phase methods than to a plain parabola, because it fits phase differences rather than the residual curve. That suggests our tracker may suffer less from coarse slices than a least-squares-plus-parabola tracker would, but **no published head-to-head of phase interpolation against least squares on the same data was found**, so this is inference. Our 100 nm step is four to five times coarser than Huhle and Kovari (20-25 nm), and the same as Lansdorp. The cheapest diagnostic for slice-interpolation error is to look for an error that repeats every 100 nm as a stuck bead is scanned through z. One note on absolute heights: LUT z is objective travel, not bead travel in water; papers apply a factor of 0.88 from the index ratio, but a measurement with a 1.49 NA objective gave 0.57 ± 0.02 (snippet level, authors unverified) ([Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/simple-experimental-methods-for-determining-the-apparent-focal-sh/)). This affects absolute extension, not the tracker comparison.

## Licences narrow the usable code to QTrk and the Saleh CUDA code

Only two open code bases are both relevant and MIT-licensed. **QTrk** (Cnossen, Dulin, Dekker) is C++/CUDA with COM, 1D cross-correlation, QI and a radial-profile ZLUT, MIT-licensed, last commit May 2021 ([QTrk](https://github.com/jcnossen/qtrk)); it is the best algorithm reference to port with credit, though building its DLL for an RTX 2060 needs a recent CUDA toolkit and updated project files (unverified). The **Saleh lab CUDA tracker** by Tabrizi is MIT-licensed and shows the classic method on the GPU: mirror correlation by cuFFT, a 3-point quadratic peak, the shift halved, and a radial profile with linear bin splitting; it ships CUDA 4.2 binaries and is a cross-check rather than a drop-in ([Tabrizi repo](https://github.com/shawntabrizi/GPU-Accelerated-Magnetic-Tweezer-Tracking-CUDA)). The companion LabVIEW BeadTracker has no licence file, so its code must not be copied ([BeadTracker](https://github.com/jcnossen/BeadTracker)).

**MagTrack** (London et al., Fishel lab) is the closest Python library: NumPy/SciPy with optional CuPy, the same code on CPU and GPU, v1.0.0 released 2026-09-15 ([MagTrack](https://github.com/7jameslondon/MagTrack); [preprint](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.10.31.685671)). Its default chain is COM, mirror auto-convolution, five iterations of multiline sub-pixel auto-convolution, radial profile and `lookup_z` with a 7-point local fit. It is **GPL-3.0**, so under the project's rule it is usable only as an independent numeric cross-check, never imported into our tracker. Its own benchmark shows that small kernels can be slower on a GPU (`lookup_z` 0.14 ms on CPU against 8.6 ms on GPU, workload size not read), which argues for our fused-kernel plan. The preprint reports about 1 nm Allan deviation for a single fixed-bead track at 100 Hz and about 0.1 nm after subtracting a second fixed bead ([London 2025](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.10.31.685671v1.full)). MagScope, its PyQt6 acquisition app, is also GPL-3.0 and useful only as an architecture example. No public code was found for the Seidel lab (Huhle), the Dulin lab, Xvin/PicoTwist or TweezPal. Kovari's MATLAB code is public but its licence was not checked.

## A fair test runs every candidate on identical frames

The comparison has two halves because no single data set measures everything. Simulated images give known truth, which is the only way to see bias, crosstalk and linearity; recorded frames give real optics and real noise, but no truth. Both halves feed the swappable-stage design: change one stage at a time, keep all others at the current method, and give every variant the same frames, the same ROI and the same calibration.

For simulation, two routes exist. A Lorenz-Mie generator (holopy, pylorenzmie) gives physically correct ring spacing with exact x, y, z, as Brouwer used with Poisson noise, interlacing, light gradients and astigmatism added ([Brouwer 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7202940/); [holopy](https://holopy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorial/calc_tutorial.html)); it models coherent light, so it suits relative tests, not absolute calibration of our LED images. The empirical route builds images from our own calibration stack: a Fourier sub-pixel shift of a slice, interpolation only between neighbouring slices, scaling to the recording's grey levels, Poisson and read noise, and rounding to 8 bits. To avoid scoring a tracker on its own LUT images, the tracker's LUT should come from even slices and the test images from odd slices (inference; no source gives this recipe for MT).

For recorded data, the main tool is the Allan deviation of each trace. On a stuck bead it falls as 1/√τ while white tracking noise dominates and rises once drift takes over; a tethered bead adds a Brownian bump near τ ≈ 1.89 times drag over stiffness, so tracker noise must be compared on stuck beads ([Lansdorp & Saleh 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3306435); [Ostrofet 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7103768)). Use the overlapping estimator with octave-spaced τ and the 2014 erratum's corrected formula ([erratum](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3979167)). Our file is 10,043 frames at 90 Hz, or 111.6 s, giving about 13 octave points and few degrees of freedom above about 10 s. Because both trackers see the same vibration and drift, their difference on the same frames is meaningful even though absolute floors are not comparable with other labs.

| Step | Data | What is varied | Metric | Pass signal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 Bias map | Simulated, stack-based and Mie | Sub-pixel offset 0-1 px in 0.05 px steps in x and y, at several z | Mean(est − true) and SD per offset; flatness of the fractional-position histogram ([Burov et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5707392/)) | Max bias well below scatter |
| S2 Crosstalk | Simulated | x shift at fixed z; z step at fixed x, y | dz/dx and dx/dz (nm per nm) | Smaller than current method |
| S3 Z linearity | Simulated, held-out slices | z ramp across the 6 µm range | Residual after linear fit; periodic error at 100 nm; behaviour in the 2 slices at each end | No 100 nm ripple; no edge blow-up |
| S4 Noise scaling | Simulated | Photon level; with and without 8-bit rounding | SD versus a numerical Cramér-Rao bound from stack derivatives (method sketch, unverified) | Near the bound |
| S5 Neighbours | Simulated | Second bead at 1.5-5 diameters | Distance at which bias starts | Same or closer than current |
| S6 Lost beads | Simulated | All of the above | Fraction of frames with error > 1 px or 200 nm, or flagged −1 | Not higher than current |
| R1 Stuck-bead noise | Recorded | Tracker variant | Allan deviation at τ = 1 frame and its minimum, per axis; stuck-pair difference divided by √2 | Lower than current |
| R2 Agreement | Recorded | Tracker variant | Per-frame difference to the current output: mean, SD, Allan deviation of the difference | White difference (noise), not slow (bias) |
| R3 Failures | Recorded | Tracker variant | Frame-to-frame jumps above a threshold; −1 rate | Not higher |
| R4 Time | Both | Tracker variant | ms per bead and per frame for 5 and 80 beads, CPU and GPU | Fits 6 ms per frame at 150 Hz |

The stuck bead is known: the owner always adds non-paramagnetic streptavidin polystyrene reference beads, and in the recorded file bead 1 is the one that does not follow the magnet (baseline table above). With a single reference bead, drift cannot be removed by subtracting a second stuck bead, so compare candidates on short-τ noise (frame-to-frame and the first Allan points), where drift is negligible. Final adoption needs more than this protocol: decision 0003 makes any result-changing swap a separate recorded decision with experimental validation, and the project's GPU acceptance rule calls for a real reference-bead experiment.

## Open gaps

The notes leave these questions open. The Gosse & Croquette 2002 full text was not read, so its filter band, slice spacing, interpolation formula and precision are unverified; the user can download it by hand. Our pixel size is nominal only (about 200 nm/px from 5.0 µm pixels, binning 2 and a 50x objective with its matching tube lens); a stage-micrometer or known-step check would confirm it before published nm figures are transferred. Our slice step (100 nm over ~6 µm) has not been tested for an error that repeats every slice; a slow piezo scan of the reference bead would measure it. The Cnossen 2014 full text was not read, so the size of the axial bias QTrk found, its Z interpolation and its iteration count are unverified. No head-to-head of QI, 3DPT and radial symmetry on the same MT images exists, and radial symmetry has never been validated on ring patterns. No experimental comparison of 100 nm against 25 nm slice steps on the same bead exists. No source quantifies 8-bit against 12-bit tracking precision in MT. The 3DPT repository name and licence, Kovari's code licence, Lansdorp 2013's own precision text and the Z method in the Tabrizi CUDA code (`fitprepandphase.h`) were not confirmed. MagTrack's benchmark workload was not read, so its timings cannot be turned into beads per second. The focal-shift factor for our objective is unmeasured, which matters for absolute extension, not for the tracker comparison.

## Conclusion

The literature does not offer a radically better tracker for a brightfield 8-bit rig; it offers a better way to remove two specific errors that our method may or may not carry. Pixel bias in XY and interpolation error between 100 nm slices in Z are both periodic, both invisible on stuck beads, and both measurable with the simulated half of the protocol before any code is ported. That ordering matters: running test S1 and S3 on the current method first tells us whether QI and spline Z are worth their cost, and if the current bias is already below its scatter, the port can stop at the double-precision clean-up.

The swappable-stage design is well matched to what the field knows, because the published wins are stage-local (QI replaces one XY stage and improves Z through crosstalk; Kovari replaces one Z interpolator). The licence situation also points one way: QTrk is the source to port, and MagTrack serves best as an outside oracle that runs the same frames through an independent code base with the same classic method.
